The NEMDE formulation ​

The National Electricity Market (NEM) is cleared by a single optimisation, the NEM Dispatch Engine (NEMDE), run by the Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO) every five minutes. These chapters explain that optimisation: what it decides, which constraints it respects, what it costs and how prices come out of it. They assume familiarity with electricity markets in general, but none with the NEM. For the market's institutions and history, see The dispatch process.

What happens every five minutes ​

The NEM is a gross pool with no day-ahead market and no central unit commitment. All energy is traded through one real-time auction, repeated 288 times a day. Each run covers one dispatch interval of five minutes and proceeds as follows.

  1. Offers are frozen. Each participant has submitted, per unit and per market, ten price bands for the trading day and a quantity in each band for every interval. Prices are fixed the day before; quantities can be changed (rebid) until shortly before the interval.

  2. The starting point is measured. AEMO reads each unit's output and each interconnector's flow from its telemetry system (SCADA) at the start of the interval.

  3. Forecasts are prepared. AEMO forecasts each region's demand at the end of the interval, and the maximum output of each wind and solar farm.

  4. Network limits are modelled. The transmission network is not modelled line by line. AEMO expresses each limit as a linear inequality over unit outputs and interconnector flows, called a generic constraint, and computes its right-hand side from current system conditions.

  5. NEMDE solves one linear programme for all five regions together. It chooses energy targets, interconnector flows and reserve enablement at least cost.

  6. Targets and prices are published. Each unit receives the output it should reach by the end of the interval. Each region receives one energy price and ten reserve prices.

Two features set the NEM apart from most other markets. Reserves, called frequency control ancillary services (FCAS), are cleared in the same optimisation as energy, because they draw on the same plant. And prices are regional: every unit in a region is settled at the price of one node, the regional reference node, adjusted by a fixed loss factor.

The optimisation in one view ​

NEMDE minimises the cost of the offers it accepts, plus a penalty for every constraint it has to violate:

Each is the MW cleared from an offer band and each is its price. Generation offers are a cost. Consumption bids are a benefit, so they enter with a minus sign. The hat on the energy prices marks their adjustment to the regional reference node. Every constraint that can be violated has a slack , priced at a penalty factor times the market price cap (MPC). is a negligible term that shares dispatch between equally priced offers.

Time conventions ​

  • A dispatch interval is identified by the time at which it ends. The interval stamped 18:00 covers 17:55 to 18:00.

  • All market times are Australian Eastern Standard Time (UTC+10) all year. The market does not observe daylight saving.

  • A trading day runs from 04:00 to 04:00 the next calendar day. Offer prices are fixed per trading day, so the interval ending 03:30 on 2 March uses offers for 1 March.

  • Ramp rates in this text are in MW per hour, so   is the MW a unit can move in one interval. Some published fields are in MW per minute; the conversion is a factor of 60.

Where the data lives ​

AEMO publishes every input and result of dispatch as tables of the MMSDM, in monthly archives. The cache built by AustralianElectricityMarketsData stores each table under its MMSDM name, one directory per archive month:

text
~/<.nemdb_cache>/<TABLE>/archive_month=YYYY-MM-01/*.parquet

Each chapter ends with a table that maps its symbols to MMSDM tables and columns. In those tables the Table column is both the MMSDM table name and the cache directory, and Time key says which row applies to an interval. Three kinds of time key occur.
